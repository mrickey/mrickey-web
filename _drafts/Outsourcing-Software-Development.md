I was talking with a friend of mine about his business. His company is a little more than a start-up, he has about 30 employees including a couple of developers. He was telling he found a company with local offices and developers overseas. They could offer project management, design, QA, everything. He felt it would be an easy way for him to put out a mobile app and not need any internal resources. Wait just one minute...no internal resources? I've been on both sides of this equation before. With two different companies, I have outsourced to overseas developers. I have also worked as a project manager for a company with project managers and designers in the US and overseas developers. I discouraged his mindset and suggested he take a more hands-on approach towards this project. In this article I'll talk about some of the best and worst practices I've seen when outsourcing software development.

## Getting Started

I strongly suggest you start an outsourced project the same way you would start an internal project. You need to identify your target market, users (create personas), and the application's functionality. Looking at competitive products is important as well. Make a list of things you like and dislike in other apps, this will help your team understand your vision. After you have a good idea what your product will do you need to gauge the size of the project. Talk to people you know...developers familiar with your platform of choice. Have them estimate the number of man-hours in the project. I recommend asking for two numbers, best-case and worst-case. Developers are always gun-shy about giving estimates, there are just too many unknowns. If you ask for a range they are a lot more comfortable, and it gives you an idea of the risk in the project. Let's talk about each of the things you need to prepare before talking to a development team about your project. - Define your target market - Having the target market defined will help with decisions during the process. It will also help to prevent an ever expanding set of requirements.
- Create persons - This is a description of each type of person that will be using your software.
- Define the functionality - What will the app do?

## Target Market

The target market will drive the design of your product. It also gives the developers a better understanding of the application's purpose. For example, if you tell the developers they are creating an app for ride sharing they might think of Uber or Lyft as a model. If your target market defines a group of people with a common interest traveling to or from a singe location, your developers will know you are thinking about car pools. Identifying the market size and location is important as well, this will help you to define the project budget. ## Users

Who will be using your application? Be specific. Personas are a great way to approach this. In a persona you identify different users of your system. You will reference them later when you create user stories. Wikipedia defines personas as "...fictional characters created to represent the different user types that might use a site, brand, or product in a similar way..." You need to be as complete as possible, find a template and use it. You may think it's obvious who the users are, but it may not be to your developers. Remember, you've been thinking about this app for a long time, they have a lot to learn before you both on the same page. Remember there is probably more than just one person that has your app on their phone. In our car pooling example, you have people offering rides, people requesting rides. Seems easy enough. But, you probably offer customer service, have system admins, and probably others. Create personas for each of these roles. You should also think about the different users for each role. There could be different types of people offering rides. There could be a college student that is looking for a car pool buddy, there could also be parents looking to share the driving responsibility with others. How they interact with the app could be different, their skill or experience using a mobile device might be different as well. ## Functionality

Your goal here is to communicate your vision to the developers. You are now actually documenting what your application will do. I suggest starting with wireframes. Use simple, hand-drawn, wireframes. Find a template online that you can print, it should have just the outline for a phone, or web page. Try to find one that has a grid of dots to make alignment easier. I do not recommend doing your wireframes on the computer. There is something about pencil on paper that makes it come to life. ![](http://35.202.40.146/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/29-sketched-ui-wireframe-300x218.jpg)Create your wireframes, name each screen, add notes that describe functionality. Stick these drawings up on the wall and walk through each process. If the flow doesn't feel good, make changes. Create documents to support the wireframes. You don't need to go very deep here, but you need to discuss algorithms, specific functionality, interfaces with other systems, etc. It is just as important to document what you know as it is to document what you don't know. If there is some functionality you want but you aren't sure how to implement it, include it in your document. Your developers may have some ideas to help. You can plan for the future here as well. You may have a vision for the initial version, and functionality that would come later. Document this in a "Future Functionality" section. If the developers know what is coming they may make different decisions about how to implement certain functionality. It is a red flag to look too far into the future. Design and build for what you know, not what might be. You want to be as complete as possible with your wireframes. Consider all of the paths a user might travel through your application. Think about potential customer service issues, could the app deal with them? Customer service calls are expensive and you should try to avoid them if possible. Consider all of the personas and roles. In your application you may need multiple mobile apps, you may also need one or more web interfaces (end user and customer service, for example). Be sure you have covered everything. Now, you've put in a lot of work here. You should feel good about this. You have probably learned a few things along the way. In many ways your project is like a newborn baby at this point. It has been shaped and formed by the parent's DNA. This infant is now ready to be raised by a community. It will receive input from many different sources. You know your project better than anyone and you have an emotional attachment to it. Now is the time to let your project mature, don't be too attached to the details of the wireframes. Let your project flow and grow into the great application it will become. This area is where I've seen a lot of frustration with projects. I've seen plenty of designs created by people that don't understand user interface (UI) or user experience (UX) guidelines or usability recommendations from platform designers, then get frustrated when the developers have difficulty creating a usable screen. All I'm saying is be open to input and suggestions. Don't try to make all of the screens match exactly between iOS, Android, Mac, and Windows. Let your developers use elements of the platform, it will be easier on your users. ## Engaging a Company

Finding a company that can take on your project isn't difficult, there are plenty around. Evaluating them can be more of a challenge. You will find that most companies will try to keep you in the sales organization until you sign a contract. I strongly recommend you visit their offices and meet with their project managers before signing a contract. The project manager will be your primary interface with the company, so you should feel confident and comfortable with them before you start. Get references and call them. Ask a lot of questions. - Where did the company excel?
- What could the company have done better?
- Talk about project management, how did they communicate project updates, how often did they talk?
- Are they doing more work with the company.
- Was the project and product ultimately successful?

Think about they type of relationship you want with this company. Is this a one-time thing, or do you plan to add features? I typically recommend planning for a long-term engagement. Any software project used internally or by your customers is going to need updates. You will refine your ideas for the app, the workflow may change, you don't use certain sections, interfaced services (like credit card processing or mapping) will change, new operating systems will be released, your users will request features you haven't thought about. You can have large team to get the initial project done, reducing the team size as you go into maintenance mode. If you shut down the entire project while you figure out what's next the developers and project manager will be assigned to other projects. You could lose all of the product knowledge and have to start with a fresh team. Some companies will offer different rates for each skill. For example, a graphic designer may be billed at a different rate than a developer or project manager. Other companies will offer a blended rate, the same hourly rate for all people working on the project. The company I worked for most recently charged a blended rate that was around $100 per hour. This may seem like a lot for an outsourced team. But, you should remember you can have fractional people in this type of a system. For example, you may be design heavy at the start then become more developer heavy as the project gets underway. You also have many types of resources available including system architects and system admins. Any of these people can be pulled into your project as needed at the same rate. You also need to decide how you want to manage your budget. You can go fixed price, but understand that you will be held to your original requirements with a strict hand. If you want to make changes there will be change orders that have to be approved. If you go with a monthly budget of hours you will have more flexibility. Next, you will talk about how you want to manage your project, the two most common choices are waterfall and scrum/agile. I'll talk about them further down in this article. You should have scans of your wireframes and documentation ready to share. If you are interfacing with other systems (credit card processing, mapping systems, or other outside systems) have that documentation available as well. This is very important, some of these sites will restrict access to users outside the US. I recommend putting these into a cloud folder you can share with the developers. ## Kicking off Your Project

Once you have selected a vendor. You will need to kick off your project. It will probably feel like you are going through this a couple of times. The company will have a process for this, the kickoff is a process to hand you off from sales to the project manager and development team. You might be asked to go through your wireframes, explain your market, or other aspects of your project. You will talk about your project, for many of the people in the room this will likely be their first exposure to your project. You will talk about how the project will be managed. I have a couple recommendations here. ![](http://35.202.40.146/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Waterfall-300x134.jpg)If you are doing a fixed price bid you should probably use waterfall. This is the traditional project management system. The primary visual for this is a Gantt Chard, a set of horizontal bars that show how long each task will take and at what point in the project it will get done. This works well for small, well defined projects. If there are a lot of unknowns it's not as great. ![](http://35.202.40.146/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/scrum-300x224.jpg)For monthly budgets I recommend Scrum. In this approach work is done in Sprints, usually two weeks. The concept here is to have a prioritized list of tasks. For each Sprint you grab about two weeks worth of tasks and focus on them. At the end of the Sprint you should have some implemented functionality you can look at and test. You continue with Sprints until you have a releasable product. You then continue with Sprints as you fix reported bugs and implement additional features. You could push a new version to the app store every two weeks...if you want to. This also gives you flexibility to change focus. You will change the priority of tasks and keep going. Bug tracking is the next thing you will talk about. You need to have a system all team members can access. You want complete visibility here. Ideally it would be tied to your project management system. I've had great success with [Jira](https://www.atlassian.com/), it is very good for distributed teams. Source control and daily builds come next. Depending on your development prowess, you may not want to go very deep here, but you should feel comfortable the company is doing a good job managing your intellectual property. Source control systems allow multiple developers to work on projects at the same time. It will help to prevent them from creating changes that conflict. It will also record all changes to the source code. If a bug is introduced they will be able to see the code before and after the change. It is a powerful tool. The most common tools are Git and Mercurial. There are hosted version available on sites like Github, Google Code, BitBucket, and many others. Even if you are not a programmer you should have access to the source control system. This will give you the ability to see comments from the developers about the changes they are making. This can usually be setup as a daily digest of activity. My favorite system is Jira from Atlassian. They have a very nice integrated system for bug tracking, project management, and source control. ## Managing Your Project

How do you keep track of your project? Your selected company will have recommendations here. Talk to them about how often they suggest meeting, how they would like to provide updates, and what tools are available to you. From my experience, if you are doing Scrum you should expect to have a short daily call with your project manager. For waterfall, you will probably have weekly calls with additional calls as needed. The following call topics stared with Scrum, but I like them for waterfall as well. Each member on the call reports with the following: - what was accomplished - this is the team members chance to let others know what was completed since the last meeting.
- what is planned - What the team member has planned before the next meeting.
- blocking items - situations preventing progress. You need to listen to these carefully, and work to resolve them. It could be something minor that will resolve itself. It could be more significant, like an associated service isn't setup yet, or the developer doesn't have credentials.

There are any number of issues that could come up during these status calls. Listen and guide the team. Make sure there are no language issues, for team members where English is a challenge, having a chat window open can help. This can be frustrating at times, stick with it, it will get better. In addition to calls I recommend using a chat platform for communications. Ideally, it would include EVERYONE on the project. Slack is a great choice as it allows good control over security and allows for channels to organize conversation. Clients are available for all of the common mobile, desktop, and web platforms. Atlassian has Stride, this integrates with Jira and offers many of the same features as Slack. Stride was released as this article was being written, it is too new to give a full recommendation. ### Daily Builds / Continuous Integration

Daily builds are just what they sound like. Each day, or several times during the day, an automated process pulls are the current source code and builds your project. This helps the developers know if there is a problem. It also allows testing to start earlier. For example, you could get a daily digest of completed work items. You can load a daily build and test the changes. This gives you confidence in what is being done, you can also show other people in the company the progress of your project. You can update work items to show your acceptance or identify issues. # Pending
